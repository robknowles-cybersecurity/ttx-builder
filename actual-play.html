The Table Runs It

What the gamebook sounds like with seven people and a clock.

The New Hire, module two · Meridian Financial · 90-minute format, Standard

YOU are reading a table at work — a fictional one. Ray facilitates. Janet has HR, Marcus the SOC, Tom is counsel, Alan the executive VP, Chris holds IT/IAM, and Dana leads the insider threat program. They are mid-exercise in The New Hire, coming back from a break with the reveals about to land.

All persons and organizations are fictional. This transcript is a teaching artifact: it models what good facilitation sounds like, including the pauses.

Ray (Facilitator): Before we pick the story back up — quick temperature check. Janet, you’ve had the hiring file in front of you since the break. One sentence.

Janet (HR): One sentence? Fine: I have a file that looked normal in April and looks radioactive in September, and nothing in it changed.

Ray: Hold that. Reading from section six. “While HR sits with the re-verification notice, the SOC’s morning queue produces two items about the same account. Six weeks of hindsight are about to arrive at once.” Marcus, these are your team’s artifacts. Take us through the analytics alert.

Marcus (SOC): Yeah. So the platform flagged account DVOSS — four point one gigs synced down from the primary repo over ten days. That’s twelve times his baseline. Then two big archives created in a folder called env-backup. No egress that we’ve seen. I want to be precise about that phrasing: no egress that the platform can see. It’s blind to removable media on that endpoint profile and it doesn’t cover two legacy repo trees.

Tom (Legal): When you say blind —

Marcus: I mean if it left the laptop by USB, we would not have an alert. We’d have nothing.

Ray: (lets it sit — one, two, three, four, five)

Alan (Exec VP): Okay, I’ll be the guy. He’s a developer shipping a platform release in six weeks. Big syncs and local archives are what crunch looks like. If you pulled my top three engineers’ telemetry right now, how many would look like this?

Marcus: Honestly? Maybe one. And that’s fair, and it’s why my Tier 3 kept digging instead of paging everyone at 2 a.m. Which brings me to the part I don’t love saying out loud.

Ray: Say it anyway. That’s the exercise.

Marcus: There’s a correlated event in the history. Forty-one days ago this account threw an impossible-travel alert. It auto-closed. VPN, low confidence, one of about forty that day. Nobody looked at it. If we’d pulled the thread then — I don’t know what we’d have found then, but we’d be having this meeting six weeks ago.

Janet: Marcus, I’m not piling on, but I need to match that with mine, because I’ve been sitting on it since the break and Ray said reveal when it’s natural. It’s natural. The hire was expedited. Recruiter flagged camera-off in two of three interviews. The hiring manager waived in-person onboarding — relocation in progress, it’s approved, it’s in the file. Week three, the replacement laptop shipped to a different state than his address of record. Week two, he split his direct deposit across two banks.

Chris (IT/IAM): And I’ll stack mine: his MFA is enrolled to a VoIP number. Passed enrollment because we don’t check number type — that’s a finding, write it down. Week one he requested a remote-collaboration tool, got denied, then asked helpdesk whether personal-device repo access was allowed.

Ray: Dana, you’ve been writing and not talking.

Dana (InT Lead): Because I’m counting. (reads) Fails re-verification. Camera avoidance. Waived in-person onboarding. Out-of-state equipment shipping. Split deposits early. VoIP MFA. Remote-tool requests. Volume anomaly on the crown jewels. And Marcus’s Tier 3 note — the persistent inbound session, the thing that looks like his laptop is being operated through a device, from a third state, on a ten-p.m.-to-six-a.m. rhythm. Individually, every one of these has an innocent version. I want to say that clearly, because I’m about to say the other thing. There’s a sector bulletin from last month — I’ve had it in my bag this whole meeting — describing remote-hire identity fraud tradecraft. It reads like someone wrote a biography of this account.

Alan: You’ve had that the whole meeting?

Dana: I’ve had it a month, Alan, and I want the room to notice why I hesitated, because it’s the same reason the alert auto-closed and the same reason the waiver got approved: he’s good. He’s the best engineer on your release. Every one of us gave a high performer the benefit of the doubt in our own lane, and nobody could see the other lanes.

Ray: I’m going to name that and put it on the improvement list exactly as said: high performance suppressed reporting in every function independently. Okay. Tom. The room is leaning. Lawyer it.

Tom: Two things, and they cut opposite directions, which is why I get paid. First: everything we have is consistent with fraud and also consistent with a guy with a weird home network mid-relocation. If we act like it’s proven and we’re wrong, that’s an employment action against a possibly innocent worker based on — Janet, help me — a vendor letter and vibes. Second, and this one keeps me up: if the fraud version is true, then payroll runs in four days, and there is a version of the world where wiring that money is itself a violation, whether or not we knew. Strict liability doesn’t grade on effort. So I want the payments frozen almost more than I want the forensics, and I want both without tipping him, and I’m aware those goals fight each other.

Ray: Which is the decision point, so let’s take it formally. Section twelve. Two postures. Contain now — suspend access today, hold pay with counsel, go to a direct conversation, accept that we tip our hand. Or monitor quietly — access substantially intact, enhanced monitoring, verify identity and scope, accept that the crown jewels stay in reach while we watch. Before anyone votes: Alan, argue containment. Dana, argue the watch. Sixty seconds each.

Alan: Seriously?

Ray: It’s the best minute of the whole exercise. Go.

Alan: …Fine. Contain, because everything we just listed says the identity is fake, and you don’t leave a fake identity holding commit access to a payment platform while lawyers verify. My release is already dead if this is real — six more days of watching doesn’t resurrect it, it just grows the blast radius I answer for. And the town-hall line about not spying on employees? Monitoring him harder is the surveillance program, we’re just doing it without admitting we’ve decided.

Ray: Dana. The watch.

Dana: Monitor, because right now we’d be containing on correlation, and the moment we suspend, we’ve told him — and whoever operates him — everything we know, and we’ve told them with our weakest evidence. Nine days of full-fidelity watching with a hard tripwire converts suspicion into a case: identity confirmed or cleared, scope established, evidence preserved in a form that survives a courtroom. If we cut access today, we may never learn what already left. And if we’re wrong about him, monitoring is the only option that lets us be wrong privately.

Ray: Votes. Contain?

(Alan. Chris. Janet, slowly.)

Ray: Monitor?

(Dana. Marcus. Tom.)

Ray: Three-three. Ties go to the senior role present, which — Alan.

Alan: (long pause) You know what convinced me? Tom’s payroll thing. I can defend a slipped release. I cannot defend the wire we sent after this meeting happened. Contain. Do it clean, Chris — what was the phrase from your sheet?

Chris: SSO, VPN, repo, email, timesheet portal. In that order. Done wrong, he gets a fifteen-minute warning.

Ray: The room chooses containment. (flips pages, grinning) Turn to section thirteen. And I’ll tell you now — at 4:11 this afternoon, HR gets an email.

Janet: Oh, I hate that you’re enjoying this.

Ray: — after the break.

End of excerpt. In the full exercise, the vote leads to Section 13 (Branch A: Containment) and the resignation inject at Section 14.

Facilitation moves worth stealing

Five moves modeled above, all of them portable to your own table:

The five-second silence after a hard reveal. Asking the reluctant discloser to say it anyway. Naming a systemic finding in the room’s own words. The sixty-second steelman swap before any vote. And letting the tie land on a player, not the facilitator.
